What a Short Sale Is

Life changes. A job ends, income drops, someone gets sick, a marriage ends, or everyday costs rise past what a paycheck covers. When that happens, a mortgage that was once manageable can become impossible to keep up with.

Most homeowners in that position do not know what their options are, and the people calling and mailing them usually want to buy the house cheaply. This guide is meant to inform you, not to pitch you.

The definition

A short sale is when your mortgage lender agrees to accept less than the full amount you owe so that the home can be sold. The home is listed and sold like any other home. The difference is that the lender has to approve the sale, because the sale price will not cover the loan.

For example, if you owe $300,000 and the home sells for $250,000, the lender is accepting $50,000 less than it is owed. That $50,000 gap is called the deficiency. Whether the lender forgives it or keeps the right to collect it is the most important question in a short sale, and page 4 answers it.

Why would a lender agree to this?

Foreclosure is slow and expensive for a lender: legal fees, months of waiting, and a home that may sit empty and lose value. A short sale usually gets the lender more money, sooner, with less risk. That is why lenders have a process for it, and why many of them will also help with your moving costs when they approve one.

What a short sale is not

It is not a way to make money from the home. You will not walk away with proceeds from the sale. It is a way out of a loan you cannot keep paying, with less damage to your credit and your future than a foreclosure, and with the terms in writing before anything is final.

The earlier you ask, the more options stay open. Nothing in this guide requires you to decide anything today. It does require that you find out where you stand before a foreclosure sale date is set, because after that point the choices narrow quickly.

Your Options as a Homeowner

A short sale is one option, not the only one. Which one fits depends on whether your hardship is temporary or permanent, whether the home is worth more or less than you owe, and how much time you have. Here is the whole list, plainly.

Loan modification

Your lender changes the terms of your existing loan so the payment is manageable. Worth asking about first if your hardship is temporary and you want to stay in the home.

Refinance

A new loan replaces the old one at better terms. This usually only works if you still have equity and your income and credit can qualify for the new loan.

Traditional sale

If the home is worth more than you owe, you can sell it, pay off the loan, and keep what is left. No lender approval is needed.

Short sale

If the home is worth less than you owe and you cannot keep paying, a short sale lets you sell with the lender's approval. Whether the remaining balance is forgiven depends on who owns your loan, and it is written in the approval.

Foreclosure

Foreclosure is what happens when none of the above happens in time. The lender takes the home and controls the outcome. Every option on this page exists to avoid it, and you can still pursue one after foreclosure has started.

Deciding between them

Two questions settle most of it. Can you realistically keep making the payment if the terms changed? If yes, ask your lender about a modification before anything else. Is the home worth less than you owe? If yes, a traditional sale is off the table and a short sale is the way to sell.

We will tell you honestly if a short sale is not the right fit, including when another option on this page is better for you. That conversation is free and carries no obligation.

Short Sale Against Foreclosure, Side by Side

Both leave a mark. The differences are in who controls the process, what you can know in advance, and how soon you can own a home again.

Waiting periods are the agencies' standard minimums; your own lender can require longer. "Extenuating circumstances" means a documented one-time event outside your control, such as a serious illness or the death of the main earner. The underwriter at your new lender decides whether yours qualifies.

Why the gap matters

For a conventional loan, which is what most buyers use, the difference is three years. That is three years sooner that you can own a home again. The other difference is knowledge: in a short sale you know the terms, including the balance and the moving help, before you commit. In a foreclosure you find out afterward.

What Your Lender Will Look For

A hardship

A change in your life that means you can no longer afford the mortgage, now or soon. Lenders accept:

  • Job loss, or a cut in income or hours
  • Illness, disability, or medical bills
  • Divorce or separation
  • The death of a co-borrower or the main earner
  • A military move or a job transfer
  • A payment increase on an adjustable-rate loan
  • A business that failed or lost income
  • Everyday costs that have risen past your income

Documents that prove it

Lenders rarely push back because a hardship is not real. They push back because the documents are missing. You will need:

  • A hardship letter. Keep it short and stick to the facts: what changed, and when.
  • Proof of income and expenses. Usually pay stubs, bank statements and tax returns.
  • Proof of the event, where there is one. A termination letter, medical records, a divorce decree, a death certificate, military orders, or a notice of a payment increase.

Once you decide to work with us, we gather these with you so nothing is left out.

Do I have to be behind on payments?

It depends on your lender. Some require at least one missed payment before they will consider a short sale. Others only need the hardship proven. Keep paying until you know what your lender requires. Missed payments hurt your credit separately from the short sale and can lengthen the wait before you can buy again. Once you decide to work with us, we contact your lender and find out exactly what it needs.

Will the lender come after me for the difference?

It depends on who owns your loan, which is not always the company you send payments to. Most loans we see are owned by Fannie Mae or Freddie Mac, and those waive the remaining balance as part of the short sale approval. FHA, VA and USDA loans usually do too, but no lender guarantees it. Other lenders decide case by case. Either way, the answer is written in the approval documents before anything is final, and if the difference is not waived, you can decide not to go ahead. Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac each have a free loan lookup on their websites.

If you have a second mortgage or a home equity line

Every lender on the home has to agree. A second lender usually wants a payment at closing before it releases its lien, and who covers that payment varies from one sale to the next. Tell us at the start. It changes the picture, and you should know that before anything else.

The Process, Step by Step

1

You contact us

A first conversation about your situation. No cost, no commitment. If a short sale is not the right fit, we say so.

2

Hardship and eligibility review

We ask about your hardship and your loan to see whether a short sale fits.

3

Documents, and your lender is told

Once you decide to go ahead, we collect what the lender needs and let it know you are pursuing a short sale. Some lenders start their review here; others wait for an offer.

4

Listed, and an offer comes in

The home goes on the MLS like any other. A buyer makes an offer that depends on the lender's approval.

5

The package goes to the lender

The offer is sent with all of your short sale documents. The lender reviews, confirms the hardship, and approves, counters or declines.

6

Approval documents, then closing

They set the terms, including whether the remaining balance is waived and any moving allowance. You see them before anything is final. Then closing is scheduled and the sale closes.

How long it takes. From your first call to an approved short sale typically takes 30 to 60 days. Closing after approval depends on the buyer and the lender, so that part varies. Getting your documents in early is the biggest thing that keeps it moving.

What it costs you. You pay us nothing out of pocket. Our fee is paid through the real estate commission at closing. The one cost that can fall on a seller is a payment a second lender asks for at closing, which is why we ask about second mortgages first.

If foreclosure has already started

Often still possible, and the earlier the better. If you have received a notice or court papers, have them ready when you call, and ask a foreclosure attorney about the case itself.
